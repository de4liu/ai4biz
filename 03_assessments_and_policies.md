# IDSC 4101: Generative and Agentic AI for Business
## Assessments, Capstone, and Course Policies

### Assessment Overview

This course uses a hybrid assessment model designed to evaluate both **conceptual understanding** and **applied thinking**. No assessment requires coding or a working software prototype. Exams are case-based and test your ability to reason about AI systems, evaluate business decisions, and apply frameworks from the course. The capstone is a team project culminating in a live pitch at the **AI Innovation Showcase** — a trade show–style event open to external practitioners and guests.

### Reflection Posts (20%)

Four short reflection posts, submitted individually via the course LMS. Each post is 200–300 words and responds to a specific prompt tied to that week's activities (prompts are listed in the weekly schedule). Reflections are not summaries — they are your honest, specific reaction to something that surprised, confused, or interested you. Grading is based on specificity and engagement, not on having the "right" answer.

**Grading criteria (each post, 5 points):**
- 5 pts: Specific, insightful, connects to course concepts or personal/professional context
- 4 pts: Specific but surface-level connection to concepts
- 3 pts: General observations without specific examples
- 2 pts: Submitted but minimal engagement
- 0 pts: Not submitted

Late posts are accepted up to one week late at half credit. Posts submitted more than one week late receive no credit.

### Midterm Conceptual Exam (25%)

**Format:** Take-home, open-note, individual. Released after Week 4 Session 2; due before Week 5 Session 1.
**Estimated time:** 60–90 minutes
**No coding required.** This exam does not ask you to write code, build workflows, or use any tools during the exam itself.

The midterm consists of **four case-based short-answer questions**, each accompanied by a brief scenario (1–2 paragraphs describing a business situation involving AI). You will be asked to analyze the situation, apply a framework or concept from the course, and recommend a course of action with reasoning.

**Topics covered:**
- How large language models work and why they behave the way they do (hallucination, context limits, temperature)
- Distinguishing generative AI from agentic AI and other AI paradigms
- Identifying automation opportunities using the frequency × effort × standardizability framework
- Evaluating AI tools for a specific business function
- Recognizing responsible AI concerns in a described deployment

**Grading:** Each question is scored on a 25-point rubric evaluating: accuracy of concept application (10 pts), quality of reasoning (10 pts), and clarity of communication (5 pts).

**Academic integrity note:** You may use your notes, readings, and course materials. You may use AI tools to check your understanding or review definitions — but your written responses must be your own analysis. Submitting AI-generated answers as your own constitutes academic dishonesty. See the AI Use Policy section below.

### Final Conceptual Exam (25%)

**Format:** Take-home, open-note, individual. Released after Week 7 Session 1; due 48 hours later.
**Estimated time:** 60–90 minutes
**No coding required.**

The final consists of **three case-based questions**, each longer and more integrative than the midterm questions. Scenarios will require you to draw on multiple weeks of course content and make a structured recommendation as if you were advising a business leader.

**Topics covered:**
- AI strategy: how organizations prioritize and sequence AI adoption
- Agentic AI systems in business: appropriate scope, guardrails, and human-in-the-loop design
- Responsible AI: identifying bias, hallucination risk, security exposure, and governance gaps in a described system
- Vendor and platform evaluation: what questions to ask, what tradeoffs to weigh
- The future of work: how AI changes skill requirements and organizational design

**Grading:** Each question scored on a 33-point rubric: concept integration (13 pts), reasoning quality (13 pts), communication clarity (7 pts).

### Team Capstone: AI Innovation Showcase (25%)

#### Overview

The capstone is the creative center of this course. Working in teams of 3–4, you will identify a real business problem, design an AI-assisted solution, and pitch it live at the **AI Innovation Showcase** — a trade show–style event held during Week 7, Session 2.

**No working prototype or live demo is required.** Your team's job is to think like an AI product designer or business strategist: define the problem clearly, design a solution thoughtfully, explain how the AI would work in practice, and articulate the business value and risks. The Showcase evaluates the quality of your thinking, not your ability to build software.

The Showcase is open to external guests — business practitioners, entrepreneurs, and industry professionals invited by the instructor. In trade show fashion, guests rotate through team stations during the session, spend 8–10 minutes with each team, ask questions, and move on. Every team presents multiple times to different visitors. This format is deliberately different from a panel presentation: you will give your core pitch several times, adapting it to different audiences, and fielding unpredictable questions from people with real professional experience. That is the skill.

#### Team Formation

Teams of 3–4 students form during Week 4, Session 2. Students may self-select teams; the instructor will assign remaining students to teams by the end of Week 4. Teams should aim for diversity of business concentration — a team with students from marketing, finance, and operations will typically produce more interesting and realistic work than a team drawn from a single major.

#### Capstone Milestones

| Milestone | Due | Deliverable |
|---|---|---|
| Team Registration | End of Week 4 | Team names + members submitted via LMS |
| Problem Statement | Week 5, Session 1 | 1 paragraph: the business problem and who it affects |
| Solution Design Sketch | Week 5, Session 2 | 1-page diagram or description of your proposed AI workflow |
| Peer Pitch Practice | Week 6, Session 2 | 3-minute practice pitch to one other team; written peer feedback exchanged |
| Final Slide Deck | Week 7, before Session 2 | 5–7 slides submitted to LMS before the Showcase begins |
| AI Innovation Showcase | Week 7, Session 2 | Live pitch and Q&A with rotating external guests |

#### What Your Pitch Should Cover

Your team's presentation at the Showcase is a structured 3-minute pitch, repeated for each visiting guest group. It should be conversational, not read from slides. The five things every strong pitch covers:

1. **The problem** — What business problem are you solving? Who experiences it, and how often? Why does it matter?
2. **The AI solution** — What does the AI do in your solution? Which tool category would power it (conversational AI, no-code automation, agentic workflow)? Walk the guest through the logic of the workflow even without showing it running.
3. **The business value** — What changes for the business or the people using this? Time saved, revenue generated, errors reduced, experience improved?
4. **What could go wrong** — What is the biggest risk or limitation of your solution? What would you do about it? (This is not a weakness — it is evidence of mature thinking.)
5. **The "what's next"** — If this were real, what would the first 90 days of deployment look like?

Guests will ask follow-up questions after your pitch. You do not need to have every answer. "That is a great question — here is how we thought about it, and here is what we are not sure about yet" is a legitimate and impressive response.

#### Slide Deck Requirements

5–7 slides submitted via LMS before the Showcase. Suggested structure:

- Slide 1: Team name, course, problem headline (one sentence)
- Slide 2: Problem — who, what, how often, why it matters
- Slide 3: Solution overview — the AI workflow described visually (diagram, flowchart, or annotated screenshot of a similar tool)
- Slide 4: Business value — specific, quantified where possible (even estimated)
- Slide 5: Responsible AI — risks and how you would address them
- Slide 6 (optional): What's next — first steps if this were real
- Slide 7 (optional): Team bios or acknowledgments

Slides are a leave-behind for guests, not a script. Design them to be readable at a glance, not dense with text.

#### Capstone Grading Rubric

| Criterion | Points | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Problem Clarity | 20 | Is the business problem well-defined, specific, and meaningful? Does the team articulate who is affected and why it matters? |
| Solution Design | 25 | Is the AI workflow logical and well-matched to the problem? Is the tool category choice appropriate? Is the thinking original? |
| Business Value Articulation | 15 | Does the team explain concretely what changes for the business or user? Is the value claim plausible and specific? |
| Responsible AI Awareness | 15 | Does the team identify at least one meaningful risk and address it credibly? |
| Pitch Quality and Q&A | 25 | Is the pitch clear, confident, and adapted to the audience? Does the team handle guest questions thoughtfully? |
| **Total** | **100** | |

#### External Guest Judge Packet

External guests at the Showcase receive a one-page briefing that includes: a short description of the course, the rubric above (simplified), and four prompt questions to use with each team:

1. What problem does this solve, and who benefits most?
2. Walk me through what the AI would actually do — step by step.
3. What could go wrong with this in a real business, and how would you handle it?
4. If you had budget and a team, what would you build first?

Guests provide qualitative feedback and engage authentically — they are not scoring teams. The instructor scores the capstone using the rubric above. Guests often provide the most memorable feedback of the semester: plan for it, and enjoy it.

### Participation and In-Class Build Sprints (10%)

Active participation is expected in every session. This is a hands-on course — attending without engaging does not meet the participation standard. Participation credit is earned through:

- Contributing to class discussions, activities, and peer feedback moments
- Completing in-class build sprints (Build Sprint 1 in Week 3; Build Sprint 2 in Week 5)
- Showing up prepared: readings done, tools set up, ready to experiment

Build sprints are completed during class time. If you miss a session during which a build sprint occurs, you may complete an equivalent individual task submitted within one week for partial credit (up to 7/10 points for that sprint).

### AI Use Policy

This course is about AI. You will use AI tools constantly, intentionally, and as a core part of the learning experience. The AI use policy for this course is therefore more nuanced than a blanket permission or prohibition.

**AI is encouraged for:**
- Exploring ideas, generating drafts, testing prompts, and experimenting with tools during class activities and build sprints
- Using course tools (Gemini, NotebookLM, Zapier, n8n, Voiceflow) to complete assignments where specified
- Reviewing concepts, checking your understanding, or generating practice questions when studying for exams
- Brainstorming, ideation, and research at any stage of the capstone project

**AI is not a substitute for your own thinking in assessments:**
- Reflection posts must represent your own observations and reasoning. Submitting AI-generated posts as your own work is academic dishonesty.
- Exam responses must be your own analysis. You may use AI to review definitions or check recall, but the reasoning, recommendation, and written response must be yours.
- In the capstone, AI tools may help you research and ideate — but the problem selection, solution design, and pitch are your team's intellectual contribution.

**Disclosure norm:** If you use an AI tool in a way that materially shaped a submitted piece of work (beyond grammar assistance), note it briefly: e.g., "I used Gemini to generate an initial outline, which I substantially revised." This is not penalized — it is professional practice. Undisclosed use that misrepresents the origin of your work is the problem, not AI use itself.

This policy reflects the norm emerging across most industries: AI fluency is expected, and so is integrity about what you contributed versus what AI generated.

### Academic Integrity

All students are expected to follow the university's academic integrity policy. Beyond the AI-specific guidance above: do not represent others' work as your own, do not share exam content with students who have not yet completed it, and cite sources in any written work drawing on external material.

### Attendance

Two or more unexcused absences will result in a one-letter-grade reduction in your final course grade. Three or more unexcused absences may result in course failure. Because this course depends heavily on in-class activity, attendance is directly tied to learning outcomes — not just a formality. Contact the instructor in advance if you anticipate a conflict.

### Accessibility and Accommodations

Students requiring academic accommodations should contact the university's disability services office and provide documentation to the instructor within the first two weeks of the course. The instructor is committed to making all course activities — including build sprints and the AI Innovation Showcase — accessible to all students.

### Late Work

Assignments submitted late without prior instructor approval are penalized 10% per day, up to a maximum of 50%. Work submitted more than five days late without approval receives no credit. Exceptions are made for documented emergencies; contact the instructor as early as possible.
