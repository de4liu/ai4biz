### Course Description

Artificial intelligence is no longer a technology story — it is a business transformation story. This course equips upper-division business students with the strategic frameworks, conceptual depth, and hands-on fluency to understand, evaluate, and deploy agentic AI in real organizational contexts. Students enter with prior experience using generative AI tools; this course accelerates past the basics to focus on where AI is going next: autonomous systems that plan, act, and coordinate without constant human direction.

Over seven weeks, students progress rapidly through advanced generative AI techniques and enterprise deployment models, then spend three full weeks on the core subject: agentic AI. Students will learn how AI agents perceive and act on their environments, how multi-agent systems coordinate complex tasks, how organizations design for human oversight and failure recovery, and how to evaluate agentic AI for real business deployment — from design through governance. The course culminates in a cross-major team capstone pitch presenting an agentic AI solution for a specific business sector.

The course integrates tools from the Google Gemini ecosystem (including multi-modal and AI Studio capabilities), no-code workflow builders such as Zapier, Make, and Relevance AI, and agentic platforms including n8n and Voiceflow. Students will also engage with the strategic and ethical dimensions of AI adoption — enterprise governance, vendor selection, security, responsible deployment, and workforce implications — preparing them for roles in which AI fluency is a baseline professional expectation.

This course is designed for students across all Carlson concentrations — Marketing, Finance, Accounting, SCO, HRIR, MIS, Entrepreneurship, International Business, and Business Analytics — and requires no prior technical or programming background.

---

### Course Rationale

The business landscape is undergoing a structural shift. Generative AI tools — large language models, image generators, and multimodal systems — have moved from novelty to operational infrastructure in under three years. Agentic AI, in which AI systems plan, take actions, and coordinate with other systems autonomously, is accelerating this shift further. According to McKinsey's 2025 State of AI report, over 70 percent of organizations are piloting or deploying generative AI in at least one business function. Roles in marketing, finance, HR, and operations now routinely require AI fluency as a baseline skill.

Yet most business graduates enter the workforce with only surface-level exposure — typically limited to using a chatbot interface. They lack the conceptual vocabulary to evaluate AI vendor claims, the workflow intuition to identify automation opportunities, and the strategic grounding to lead or contribute to AI initiatives. This course closes that gap. It is explicitly designed for business majors who will manage AI tools, commission AI projects, and make strategic decisions about AI adoption — not for those who will build AI systems from scratch. The goal is informed, confident fluency: the ability to understand what AI can and cannot do, translate business problems into AI solutions, and lead responsibly in AI-augmented organizations.

---

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Explain** the foundational mechanics of large language models and generative AI systems — including tokenization, training, inference, and hallucination — using accurate, non-technical language appropriate for business communication.

2. **Distinguish** among AI paradigms (rule-based systems, predictive ML, generative AI, and agentic AI), identifying which paradigm is appropriate for a given business problem and what constraints each carries.

3. **Design and execute** no-code AI-assisted workflows using automation platforms, connecting AI outputs to real business processes in marketing, operations, finance, or HR without writing code.

4. **Evaluate** agentic AI systems — including multi-agent architectures, tool-using agents, and AI copilots — by analyzing their reasoning patterns, failure modes, and appropriate scope of autonomy within an organizational context.

5. **Assess** the strategic, ethical, and governance implications of AI adoption in a business context, including workforce impact, bias and fairness risks, regulatory considerations, and vendor evaluation frameworks.

6. **Apply** a structured business problem-solving process to identify, scope, prototype, and present an AI-assisted solution to a real organizational challenge using tools covered in the course.

### Required Tools and Platform Access

This course uses no-cost or student-tier access to the following categories of tools. No software purchase is required; all platforms have free tiers sufficient for coursework. Specific setup instructions and access links will be distributed in the first week.

**Conversational AI and Multimodal AI (Weeks 1–4)**  
The Google Gemini ecosystem — including Gemini (conversational AI), NotebookLM (AI-powered research assistant), and Google AI Studio (prompt and model experimentation) — serves as the primary AI interface for the first half of the course. Students will use these tools to understand how large language models respond to different types of prompts, how context shapes output, and how multimodal inputs (text, image, document) change AI behavior. All tools are accessible via a standard Google account. Students without a Google account will need to create one before the first session.

**No-Code AI Workflow Builders (Weeks 3–5)**  
Platforms in this category — including Zapier, Make (formerly Integromat), and Relevance AI — allow users to connect AI capabilities to other software systems (email, spreadsheets, CRMs, calendars, databases) through a visual drag-and-drop interface. Students will use these tools to build automated workflows that incorporate AI decision-making without writing code. Free tiers on all three platforms are sufficient for course assignments. Students will choose one platform to use for their capstone project.

**Agentic AI and Conversational Agent Platforms (Weeks 5–6)**  
Platforms in this category — including n8n (workflow automation with agentic logic) and Voiceflow (conversational agent builder) — enable the construction of multi-step, goal-directed AI workflows and dialogue-based agents. These tools represent the frontier of accessible, no-code agentic AI. Students will use these platforms to build and test simple AI agents that can take sequential actions, branch based on outputs, and interact with users. Free tiers or educational access will be confirmed at course start.

**Note on Tool Evolution:** The AI tool landscape evolves rapidly. The platforms listed above are current as of course launch; the instructor reserves the right to substitute equivalent tools if access, pricing, or functionality changes materially. The conceptual skills developed in this course — workflow design, agent architecture, prompt strategy, governance evaluation — transfer across specific platforms.

### How to Read This Schedule

Each week has a unifying theme and two 100-minute sessions. **Session 1** typically introduces concepts and tools through demonstration and guided exploration. **Session 2** builds on that foundation with a hands-on activity, case discussion, or team build sprint. Readings listed under each week should be completed **before** Session 1 unless otherwise noted. Tools are introduced progressively — do not worry about mastering earlier tools before new ones appear; the course is designed to layer skills.

The arc of the course follows a deliberate progression:

- **Week 1:** Advanced GenAI foundations — multi-modal capabilities, RAG concepts, and enterprise deployment. Assumes prior basic prompting experience; moves fast into business-critical depth.
- **Weeks 2–3:** AI for business — workflow automation, no-code tools, and functional applications across marketing, finance, operations, and HR.
- **Weeks 4–6:** Agentic AI — three full weeks on autonomous systems, multi-agent architectures, human-in-the-loop design, failure modes, and real business deployment.
- **Week 7:** Strategy, ethics, and synthesis — responsible AI, organizational decision-making, and capstone presentations.

## Week 1: Welcome to the AI Era — What Is Generative AI and Why Does It Matter?

**Theme:** Orientation, curiosity, and first contact with generative AI tools.

**Session 1 — The New Landscape (Day 1)**

*Topics:*
- Course orientation: what we will build, what we will understand, how we will be assessed
- What generative AI actually is — and is not: distinguishing it from search engines, calculators, and traditional software
- A brief, non-technical mental model: how AI learns from text and generates responses (no math; think of it as a very well-read assistant that predicts what to say next)
- The generative AI moment: why 2022–2025 was a hinge point in technology history
- Live demo: instructor uses Gemini live in class to answer a business question, draft a memo, and make a factual error — students observe and discuss

*Activity:* "First Contact" — students open Gemini for the first time and complete three tasks: ask a question in their area of interest, give the AI a bad prompt, then a better one, and compare outputs. No setup beyond a Google account.

*Discussion:* What surprised you? What did it get right? What did it get wrong?

*Tools Introduced:* Google Gemini (conversational interface)

**Session 2 — Personal AI: Making Your Own Work Life Better**

*Topics:*
- Generative AI as a personal productivity tool — not a replacement for thinking, but an accelerant
- Use cases every business student can use today: drafting emails, summarizing documents, preparing for interviews, building study guides, researching companies before a job application
- Prompting as communication: the difference between a bad prompt and a great one is not magic — it is clarity, context, and specificity
- Introduction to prompt patterns: role prompting, step-by-step instructions, asking for multiple options, giving examples

*Activity:* "Upgrade Your Workflow" — each student picks one recurring task from their academic or personal life (weekly recap email, meeting agenda, study notes, cover letter draft) and uses Gemini to build a reusable prompt template for it. Students share their best templates with the class.

*Readings (before Session 1):*
- Mollick, E. "Using AI to Make Work More Meaningful" — *Harvard Business Review*, 2024
- Assigned briefing: "What is a Large Language Model?" (NotebookLM-generated summary, provided via LMS — students will evaluate it critically in class)

*Assignment Due:* None (week 1 is orientation)

## Week 2: Going Deeper — Understanding AI Well Enough to Use It Confidently

**Theme:** Building just enough conceptual foundation to be an informed, critical user — not a builder of models, but someone who understands why AI behaves the way it does.

**Session 1 — Why Does AI Do That?**

*Topics:*
- Tokens, context windows, and why AI "forgets" things in long conversations
- Hallucination: what it is, why it happens structurally, and how to reduce it — this is not a bug to be fixed, it is a feature of how the technology works
- Temperature and creativity: why AI gives different answers to the same question
- Multimodal AI: what changes when AI can see images, read PDFs, and hear audio
- Model differences: what it means when a tool says it uses a "more capable" or "faster" model, and how to think about choosing tools

*Activity:* "Break the AI" — structured red-teaming exercise. Teams are given a list of prompting strategies (adding false context, asking the AI to roleplay, pushing it toward confident wrong answers) and test Gemini's behavior. Teams report findings. Discussion: what does this tell us about how to use AI responsibly?

*Session 2 — NotebookLM: AI as a Research and Knowledge Partner**

*Topics:*
- Introduction to NotebookLM: AI that reads your documents and lets you ask questions about them
- Use cases: summarizing a 40-page report, extracting key arguments from a case study, generating a briefing document for a client meeting, building a study guide from lecture notes
- Limits: NotebookLM only knows what you give it — and it can still be wrong about what it reads
- Ideation use case: using AI to pressure-test a business idea by uploading competitor websites, industry reports, and your own notes

*Activity:* "The Analyst Sprint" — each student uploads a set of documents relevant to their concentration (a 10-K, a case study, an industry report — provided) to NotebookLM and builds a one-page executive briefing by querying the AI. Teams compare their briefings and identify discrepancies.

*Tools Introduced:* Google NotebookLM, Google AI Studio (first look — used for prompt experimentation)

*Readings (before Session 1):*
- Assigned article: "The Hallucination Problem and What to Do About It" — *MIT Technology Review*, 2024
- Optional: Suleyman, M. *The Coming Wave*, Chapter 1 (recommended text)

*Assignment Due:* **Reflection Post 1** — 200–300 words: Describe one moment this week when the AI surprised you (positively or negatively). What does that surprise tell you about how the tool works?

## Week 3: AI for Teams — Automating the Work Nobody Wants to Do

**Theme:** From personal tools to team workflows. Students learn to connect AI to the systems businesses already use — without writing code.

**Session 1 — Workflow Thinking: What Gets Automated and What Does Not**

*Topics:*
- The automation opportunity: which business tasks are repetitive, rule-based, and time-consuming enough to automate?
- A framework for identifying automation candidates: frequency × effort × standardizability
- Introduction to no-code workflow builders: what they are, how they work visually, what they can connect (email, spreadsheets, CRMs, Slack, databases, AI models)
- Demonstration: building a simple Zapier workflow that takes a form submission, passes it to Gemini for a summary, and emails the result — live in class, no code

*Activity:* "Automation Audit" — in teams, students map the workflows of a hypothetical small business (provided scenario) and identify the top 3 candidates for AI-assisted automation. Teams present their reasoning using the frequency × effort × standardizability framework.

*Session 2 — Build Sprint: Your First Automation**

*Topics:*
- Getting set up with Zapier or Make (students choose their preferred platform)
- The anatomy of a no-code workflow: triggers, actions, filters, and AI steps
- Common business automations: lead qualification, meeting follow-up drafting, customer feedback summarization, content repurposing, invoice processing
- Relevance AI: building AI-powered assistants that follow business-specific rules

*Activity:* **Build Sprint 1** — each student builds a working automation connecting at least two tools with an AI step in the middle. Examples: a Google Form that summarizes responses with Gemini and posts to a shared Slack channel; an email inbox watcher that drafts replies using AI. Instructor circulates; peer help encouraged.

*Tools Introduced:* Zapier (or Make), Relevance AI (introduction)

*Readings (before Session 1):*
- McKinsey & Company: "The State of AI in 2025" — assigned excerpt (provided via LMS)
- Case study: "Automation at Scale: How [Company] Redesigned Its Customer Service Workflow" (HBS case — to be confirmed)

*Assignment Due:* **Reflection Post 2** — 200–300 words: What workflow did you build? What broke, and how did you fix it? What would you automate next?

## Week 5: Agentic AI — Building Systems That Act, Not Just Answer

**Theme:** Crossing from generative AI (AI that responds) to agentic AI (AI that acts). Students learn what agents are, how they reason, and how businesses are beginning to deploy them.

**Session 1 — What Is an AI Agent?**

*Topics:*
- The key difference: generative AI responds; agentic AI plans, takes actions, and loops back
- Agent anatomy without code: goals, tools, memory, reasoning loops — explained through analogy and diagram
- Types of agents students will encounter in business: task agents, research agents, customer-facing conversational agents, multi-agent systems (one agent manages others)
- Agentic AI design patterns: reflection (agent checks its own output), tool use (agent calls external systems), planning (agent breaks a goal into steps), handoff (agent routes to human or another agent)
- Real business examples: AI SDR (sales development representative) that researches, drafts, and sends outreach; AI operations agent that monitors inventory and flags exceptions; AI onboarding agent that answers new employee questions

*Activity:* "Agent Blueprint" — teams sketch an agent for a business use case they find interesting. They define: What is the agent's goal? What tools does it need access to? When should it hand off to a human? What could go wrong?

**Session 2 — Build Sprint: Your First Agent with n8n or Voiceflow**

*Topics:*
- Introduction to n8n and Voiceflow: what each is best for (n8n for workflow-based agents; Voiceflow for conversational agents)
- Building a simple agent: a customer FAQ bot that can look up answers, escalate when it does not know, and log interactions
- Introduction to the concept of "guardrails": how to scope what an agent is and is not allowed to do

*Activity:* **Build Sprint 2** — teams build a simple agent using n8n or Voiceflow. Suggested builds: a customer service agent for a fictional business, a meeting scheduler agent, or a research agent that searches and summarizes. Teams demo their agent to another team at the end of the session.

*Tools Introduced:* n8n, Voiceflow

*Readings (before Session 1):*
- Assigned article: "The Rise of AI Agents in Enterprise" — *MIT Technology Review*, 2025 (provided via LMS)

*Assignment Due:* **Reflection Post 3** — 200–300 words: What does your agent do? What decision did you make about what it should NOT be allowed to do, and why?

### Reflection Posts (20%)

Four short reflection posts, submitted individually via the course LMS. Each post is 200–300 words and responds to a specific prompt tied to that week's activities (prompts are listed in the weekly schedule). Reflections are not summaries — they are your honest, specific reaction to something that surprised, confused, or interested you. Grading is based on specificity and engagement, not on having the "right" answer.

**Grading criteria (each post, 5 points):**
- 5 pts: Specific, insightful, connects to course concepts or personal/professional context
- 4 pts: Specific but surface-level connection to concepts
- 3 pts: General observations without specific examples
- 2 pts: Submitted but minimal engagement
- 0 pts: Not submitted

Late posts are accepted up to one week late at half credit. Posts submitted more than one week late receive no credit.

**Format:** Take-home, open-note, individual. Released after Week 4 Session 2; due before Week 5 Session 1.

**Academic integrity note:** You may use your notes, readings, and course materials. You may use AI tools to check your understanding or review definitions — but your written responses must be your own analysis. Submitting AI-generated answers as your own constitutes academic dishonesty. See the AI Use Policy section below.

### Team Capstone: AI Innovation Showcase (25%)

#### Overview

The capstone is the creative center of this course. Working in teams of 3–4, you will identify a real business problem, design an AI-assisted solution, and pitch it live at the **AI Innovation Showcase** — a trade show–style event held during Week 7, Session 2.

**No working prototype or live demo is required.** Your team's job is to think like an AI product designer or business strategist: define the problem clearly, design a solution thoughtfully, explain how the AI would work in practice, and articulate the business value and risks. The Showcase evaluates the quality of your thinking, not your ability to build software.

The Showcase is open to external guests — business practitioners, entrepreneurs, and industry professionals invited by the instructor. In trade show fashion, guests rotate through team stations during the session, spend 8–10 minutes with each team, ask questions, and move on. Every team presents multiple times to different visitors. This format is deliberately different from a panel presentation: you will give your core pitch several times, adapting it to different audiences, and fielding unpredictable questions from people with real professional experience. That is the skill.

#### Team Formation

Teams of 3–4 students form during Week 4, Session 2. Students may self-select teams; the instructor will assign remaining students to teams by the end of Week 4. Teams should aim for diversity of business concentration — a team with students from marketing, finance, and operations will typically produce more interesting and realistic work than a team drawn from a single major.

#### Capstone Milestones

| Milestone | Due | Deliverable |
|---|---|---|
| Team Registration | End of Week 4 | Team names + members submitted via LMS |

#### What Your Pitch Should Cover

Your team's presentation at the Showcase is a structured 3-minute pitch, repeated for each visiting guest group. It should be conversational, not read from slides. The five things every strong pitch covers:

1. **The problem** — What business problem are you solving? Who experiences it, and how often? Why does it matter?
2. **The AI solution** — What does the AI do in your solution? Which tool category would power it (conversational AI, no-code automation, agentic workflow)? Walk the guest through the logic of the workflow even without showing it running.
3. **The business value** — What changes for the business or the people using this? Time saved, revenue generated, errors reduced, experience improved?
4. **What could go wrong** — What is the biggest risk or limitation of your solution? What would you do about it? (This is not a weakness — it is evidence of mature thinking.)
5. **The "what's next"** — If this were real, what would the first 90 days of deployment look like?

Guests will ask follow-up questions after your pitch. You do not need to have every answer. "That is a great question — here is how we thought about it, and here is what we are not sure about yet" is a legitimate and impressive response.

#### Slide Deck Requirements

5–7 slides submitted via LMS before the Showcase. Suggested structure:

- Slide 1: Team name, course, problem headline (one sentence)
- Slide 2: Problem — who, what, how often, why it matters
- Slide 3: Solution overview — the AI workflow described visually (diagram, flowchart, or annotated screenshot of a similar tool)
- Slide 4: Business value — specific, quantified where possible (even estimated)
- Slide 5: Responsible AI — risks and how you would address them
- Slide 6 (optional): What's next — first steps if this were real
- Slide 7 (optional): Team bios or acknowledgments

Slides are a leave-behind for guests, not a script. Design them to be readable at a glance, not dense with text.

### AI Use Policy

This course is about AI. You will use AI tools constantly, intentionally, and as a core part of the learning experience. The AI use policy for this course is therefore more nuanced than a blanket permission or prohibition.

**AI is encouraged for:**
- Exploring ideas, generating drafts, testing prompts, and experimenting with tools during class activities and build sprints
- Using course tools (Gemini, NotebookLM, Zapier, n8n, Voiceflow) to complete assignments where specified
- Reviewing concepts, checking your understanding, or generating practice questions when studying for exams
- Brainstorming, ideation, and research at any stage of the capstone project

**AI is not a substitute for your own thinking in assessments:**
- Reflection posts must represent your own observations and reasoning. Submitting AI-generated posts as your own work is academic dishonesty.
- Exam responses must be your own analysis. You may use AI to review definitions or check recall, but the reasoning, recommendation, and written response must be yours.
- In the capstone, AI tools may help you research and ideate — but the problem selection, solution design, and pitch are your team's intellectual contribution.

**Disclosure norm:** If you use an AI tool in a way that materially shaped a submitted piece of work (beyond grammar assistance), note it briefly: e.g., "I used Gemini to generate an initial outline, which I substantially revised." This is not penalized — it is professional practice. Undisclosed use that misrepresents the origin of your work is the problem, not AI use itself.

This policy reflects the norm emerging across most industries: AI fluency is expected, and so is integrity about what you contributed versus what AI generated.
