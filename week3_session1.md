### SLIDE 2 — The Ideation Mindset: A Shift in the Question

**Title:** Stop Asking "How Does It Work?" — Start Asking "What Would It Need to Do?"

**Bullets:**
- Most people's first AI question is technical: "How is this built?"
- The business question is different: "What decision or task does this replace, augment, or accelerate — and what is that worth?"
- The ideation mindset: look at a function, find where time, judgment, or creativity is spent on high-volume, repetitive, or data-heavy work — that is the AI opportunity
- You do not need to know how to build it to have a strong opinion about whether it's worth building
- Today's framework: **Where is the AI opportunity? → What would it need to do? → What value does it create? → What are the risks?**

**Speaker Notes:**
This is the most important slide of the session and I want to spend a few minutes on it before we dive into the functions. The reason so many business leaders are confused about AI is that they approach it like engineers — "how does this work, can my team build it?" That's the wrong question for most of the room. The right question is the business question: where is the value, what problem does it solve, and what would the AI system need to do to solve it? You don't need to understand transformer architecture to have a credible AI strategy. You do need to understand the business well enough to spot where AI has leverage. That's what today is about. We're going to practice this across three functions: marketing, sales, and customer experience. Every slide follows the same pattern: here's how the function works, here's where AI lands, here's the ideation framework in action.

### SLIDE 3 — AI in Marketing: The Landscape

**Title:** Marketing Was Built for AI — High Volume, Data-Rich, Repetitive Creativity

**Bullets:**
- Marketing involves enormous volumes of content, targeting decisions, and measurement — all of which scale well with AI
- **Content generation:** first drafts of copy, email subject lines, ad variants, social posts, landing pages — AI produces volume; humans curate quality
- **Audience segmentation:** AI identifies patterns in customer behavior that humans would miss — grouping audiences by likely response, not just demographics
- **Personalization at scale:** the same message, customized for thousands of micro-segments — not one email blast, but 500 personalized variants
- **A/B testing with AI assistance:** AI generates test variants, reads results, and suggests what to try next — compressing the iteration cycle from weeks to days
- **Search and SEO:** AI tools analyze keyword gaps, competitor content, and search intent — and draft content that targets identified opportunities

**Speaker Notes:**
Marketing is probably the function where AI has moved fastest, partly because the risk of a bad AI output in marketing is lower than in, say, finance or legal — you review content before it goes live. That tolerance for human-in-the-loop review has made marketing a natural proving ground for generative AI. The key theme across all of these use cases is volume: AI doesn't replace the creative judgment of a marketing director, but it absolutely replaces the junior copywriter's first-draft production work. A team of three marketers with good AI tools can now produce the content output that previously required a team of ten. That has enormous implications for team design, not just productivity.

### SLIDE 4 — Marketing: The AI Opportunity Framework in Action

**Title:** Spotting the Marketing AI Opportunity — The Four-Question Test

**Bullets:**
- **Q1: Is this task high-frequency?** Does the team do this daily, weekly, or for every campaign? If yes — AI leverage is high.
- **Q2: Is the output standardizable?** Can you define what "good" looks like clearly enough to prompt it? Content, summaries, reports, subject lines — yes. Brand strategy — less so.
- **Q3: Does it require real-time data?** If the task requires current data (live pricing, trending topics, recent competitor moves) — AI needs to be connected to those data sources, not just prompted.
- **Q4: What is the cost of a bad output?** If a bad AI output reaches a customer before review, what happens? Low cost — automate with review gate. High cost — keep human more involved.

**Apply it:** Email subject line optimization → High frequency ✓ | Standardizable ✓ | No live data needed ✓ | Low cost of error ✓ → Strong AI automation candidate

**Speaker Notes:**
This is the ideation framework we'll use across all three functions today and into the functional activity later. Run any marketing task through these four questions and you'll quickly identify whether AI makes sense, what form it should take, and what the guardrails need to be. Let's practice it on one more example before we move on: brand voice development. Is it high-frequency? Not really — this is a once-or-twice-a-decade project. Is it standardizable? Partly — you can give AI examples of on-brand copy and ask it to analyze patterns. But does the output require real human judgment and taste? Yes. Cost of error? Very high — a brand that sounds like a robot permanently loses customer trust. Verdict: AI can assist in brand voice work, but this is an AI-augmented task, not an AI-automated one. That's a nuanced but important distinction.

### SLIDE 5 — AI in Sales: From Gut Feel to Data-Driven Velocity

**Title:** Sales Is Becoming a Data Sport — And AI Is the Scoreboard

**Bullets:**
- **Lead scoring:** AI analyzes hundreds of behavioral signals — website visits, email opens, content downloads, company size, industry — and predicts which leads are most likely to convert. Replaces gut-feel prioritization.
- **Outreach personalization:** AI researches a prospect (company news, LinkedIn, role changes, recent announcements) and drafts a personalized first email — in seconds, at scale
- **Call summarization and CRM logging:** AI listens to sales calls (with consent), generates a summary, and auto-populates the CRM — eliminating the post-call admin that eats 20–30% of a sales rep's time
- **Pipeline forecasting:** AI uses historical patterns and current deal signals to predict which deals will close this quarter — and which are at risk
- **Objection preparation:** AI generates likely objections for a prospect based on their profile and suggests responses — coaching tool for junior reps

**Speaker Notes:**
The biggest time sink in most sales organizations is not the selling — it's everything around the selling. Research before a call. Logging activity after a call. Writing follow-up emails. Updating the CRM. Forecasting for the weekly pipeline review. Conservative estimates suggest top sales reps spend 30–40% of their time on non-selling activities. AI can eliminate most of that, returning that time to what only humans can do well: building relationships, navigating complex negotiations, and reading the room. That's the real value proposition of AI in sales — not replacing reps, but making the time they spend actually talking to customers matter more. The lead scoring example is worth dwelling on: companies that have deployed AI lead scoring typically report that reps spend less time on unqualified leads and close rates go up — not because reps got better, but because they stopped chasing the wrong people.

### SLIDE 6 — AI in Sales: The Ideation Opportunity

**Title:** Where Are the Sales AI Wins? — Three Archetypes

**Bullets:**
- **Archetype 1: The Research-to-Outreach Workflow** — AI researches prospect in 30 seconds, drafts personalized email, rep reviews and sends. Tools: Gemini + Clay + HubSpot. Value: 10x outreach volume, same rep headcount.
- **Archetype 2: The CRM Auto-Log** — AI listens to call (Otter, Gong, or similar), transcribes, summarizes key points, extracts next steps, and logs to CRM automatically. Tools: Gong, Otter.ai + Zapier + Salesforce. Value: 2 hours of admin time back per rep per day.
- **Archetype 3: The Intelligent Pipeline** — AI flags deals at risk based on engagement signals (prospect hasn't opened emails in 10 days, meeting rescheduled twice, champion went quiet), triggers alerts to rep and manager. Tools: Salesforce Einstein, HubSpot AI, or custom agent. Value: fewer surprise misses at quarter end.

**Discussion question:** Which archetype would have the highest ROI for a 10-person B2B sales team? What data would you need to know to answer that?

**Speaker Notes:**
The discussion question is real — don't rush past it. These three archetypes represent genuinely different value propositions and different implementation complexity. The research-to-outreach workflow is the quickest to implement and has the most visible output, but its value depends on whether the team is actually volume-constrained. The CRM auto-log has massive quality-of-life improvement for reps but requires buy-in from the reps themselves — reps who feel surveilled by call recording tools sometimes push back hard. The intelligent pipeline tool has the highest executive-level appeal but requires clean, consistent CRM data to work. If your CRM data is messy — which it usually is — the AI's predictions are only as good as the inputs. That's a data quality problem masquerading as an AI problem. Important distinction for the exam.

### SLIDE 7 — AI in Customer Experience: Chatbots vs. Agents

**Title:** Not All Customer-Facing AI Is the Same — Know the Difference

**Bullets:**
- **FAQ Chatbot (Gen 1):** Rule-based, decision-tree logic. Recognizes keywords, routes to scripted response. Fast to build, brittle in practice. "Press 1 for billing, press 2 for returns" — but slightly smarter.
- **GenAI Chatbot (Gen 2):** Language model-powered. Understands natural language, handles variation in phrasing, produces fluent conversational responses. Can handle a much wider range of questions — but can also hallucinate or go off-script.
- **AI Customer Service Agent (Gen 3):** Multi-step, tool-using, action-taking. Can look up an order, check inventory, initiate a refund, schedule a callback — all within a guardrailed system. This is agentic AI in customer experience.
- The key question is not "chatbot or human?" but "what decisions can this system make autonomously, and where should it escalate?"

**Speaker Notes:**
This distinction matters enormously in Week 4 when we get into agentic AI design, so I want to plant it firmly now. The mistake most businesses make is deploying a Gen 2 chatbot — which can talk fluently — in a context that requires Gen 3 action-taking. The chatbot tells a customer "I understand you want a refund" but can't actually issue one. So the customer is routed to a human anyway, but now frustrated by a chatbot experience that felt like it was going somewhere. The design question is always: what can this system DO, not just SAY? If the answer is "only say things," you have a GenAI chatbot. If the answer is "look things up, take actions, escalate on specific conditions," you have an agent. We'll build these in Weeks 4–5.

### SLIDE 8 — AI in Customer Experience: Where It Wins

**Title:** Three Customer Experience Areas Where AI Has Real Leverage

**Bullets:**
- **Sentiment analysis at scale:** AI reads every customer support ticket, review, and feedback form and classifies sentiment, urgency, and topic category — automatically. A team of 2 can monitor what previously required a team of 15.
- **Support ticket routing:** AI classifies the issue and routes it to the right team (billing, technical, returns) without a human triage step. Reduces first-response time from hours to seconds.
- **Self-service resolution:** AI handles the high-volume, low-complexity issues that represent 60–70% of support volume (order status, password reset, return policy, store hours) — freeing human agents for the complex, high-emotion cases where humans genuinely add value.
- The CX AI opportunity: resolve the easy fast, escalate the hard thoughtfully, and learn from every interaction to improve both.

**Speaker Notes:**
The 60–70% number is important context. In most customer service operations, the large majority of incoming contacts are simple, repetitive questions with a clear right answer. "Where is my order?" "What is your return policy?" "Can I change my reservation?" These do not require human judgment — they require access to data and a clear policy. AI can handle these faster and more consistently than humans can. The strategic move is to let AI own the routine so that human agents can focus on the edge cases that require empathy, nuanced judgment, and accountability. When a customer is furious and threatening to churn, you do not want them talking to a chatbot. When a customer wants to know their order status, you absolutely do not need a human for that. The design challenge is building the escalation logic correctly — which we'll cover in depth in Week 4.

### SLIDE 11 — Activity: The 90-Second Pitch Format

**Title:** How to Pitch an AI Opportunity — The Format

**The pitch structure (90 seconds, no slides needed):**

1. **The function and the problem (20 sec):** "I'm planning to work in [function]. One of the most time-consuming and repetitive tasks is [specific task]."
2. **The AI opportunity (30 sec):** "An AI system that [does what specifically] would allow the team to [outcome], which currently takes [how long / how many people]."
3. **The value claim (20 sec):** "If this worked, you'd expect [quantified or directional benefit — time saved, volume increased, error reduced, cost avoided]."
4. **The risk check (20 sec):** "The biggest risk to watch is [specific risk — data quality, hallucination in a high-stakes context, employee pushback, regulatory constraint]."

**Partner challenge:** After the pitch, the partner asks: "What happens when the AI produces a wrong output? Who catches it, and what are the consequences?"

**Speaker Notes:**
The partner challenge question is the one that separates superficial ideation from serious thinking. Any AI deployment will, at some point, produce a wrong or unexpected output. The question is not whether — it's who catches it, how fast, and what the consequences are. A junior marketer who reviews AI-generated email copy before it sends can catch a tone-deaf subject line. An AI that automatically approves loan applications has no such gate. The challenge question forces students to think about the guardrail question before they've even finished pitching the opportunity — which is exactly the order of operations you want from a responsible business leader.

### Activity: Function Mapping + 90-Second AI Opportunity Pitch

**Purpose:** Students apply the ideation framework to a business function they personally plan to work in. The 90-second pitch format builds communication discipline: no deck, no jargon, specific claim, specific risk. Peer challenge builds the habit of anticipating failure modes before they arise.

**Duration:** 35 minutes total
- Individual work: 10 minutes
- Pair pitch and challenge: 10 minutes (5 minutes per person per pair)
- Class sharing and instructor feedback: 15 minutes (4–5 pitches, 3 minutes each including feedback)

**Materials:**
- Slide 10 worksheet (on screen) — students complete in their own doc or on paper
- No submission required — this is a practice exercise; Reflection Post 3 is the formal output

**Setup:**
- Students can self-select pairs, or instructor pairs by proximity
- Encourage pairing across majors where possible — a finance major and a marketing major will challenge each other more usefully than two marketing majors
- Instructor circulates during the 10-minute individual work phase — intervening where needed to move students from vague to specific

**Facilitation notes:**

*During individual work (10 minutes):*
Walk the room. The most common issues: (1) Students pick a function that is too broad ("AI in finance") instead of a task ("AI that extracts key figures from 10-K filings and formats them into a comparison table"). Redirect: "What's a specific thing you or someone in that job does every day that's repetitive?" (2) Students pick a task where AI is already widely deployed (email drafting) without knowing it. That's fine — the exercise is about applying the framework, not novelty. (3) Students get stuck on the value claim because they don't know the numbers. Encourage approximation: "If a sales rep spends 2 hours a day on CRM logging and AI eliminates 80% of that, that's 1.5 hours a day — what's that worth across 50 reps?"

*During pair pitch (10 minutes):*
Circulate and listen. The challenge question ("what happens when the AI produces a wrong output?") is the most important moment. If a partner is being too easy on the pitcher — "yeah that sounds great" — prompt them: "Ask them about the error case. What if the AI gets it wrong?"

*During class sharing (15 minutes):*
Call on 4–5 students who had strong pitches or interesting challenges. Prioritize functional diversity — try to feature at least three different functions. For each pitch: let the student deliver it in full (90 seconds), then give specific feedback on: (1) How specific and measurable was the value claim? (2) How serious and specific was the risk identification? (3) What would you need to know to decide whether to fund this?

**Instructor preparation:**
- Have one strong example pitch prepared to model the format before students start (or model it in the first 2 minutes of the activity setup)
- Example: "I plan to work in consulting. One of the most time-consuming tasks early in a project is building an initial market landscape — reading 15–20 industry reports and synthesizing them into a one-page briefing. An AI system that ingests a set of PDF reports and extracts the key trends, market size figures, and competitor moves would allow a consulting team to cut landscape synthesis from 2 days to 2 hours. Across a 6-month engagement with five such deliverables, that's 10 days of associate time recovered per engagement. The biggest risk: AI might summarize a report incorrectly or miss a nuance that changes the client recommendation. The guardrail is a human review step before the briefing goes to the client — AI drafts, analyst reviews."

**Debrief questions (for full group):**
1. Across all the pitches, what was the most common category of AI opportunity? (Pattern recognition question — usually high-volume content or data processing)
2. What was the most creative pitch you heard in your pair that didn't get shared with the class?
3. What was the hardest part of making the value claim specific?
4. Which risk category came up most — data quality, error consequences, employee resistance, or regulatory constraint?

**Connection to capstone:** The ideation practice in this activity directly prepares students for their capstone pitch in Week 7. The 90-second format, the value claim structure, and the risk analysis are all components of the capstone rubric. Mention this explicitly.
